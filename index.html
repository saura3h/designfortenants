The Tenant's Guide to Interior Design

A rented home always feels temporary.

The floors, the lights, the kitchen - all chosen by someone else. You rent it but every change is a conversation with your landlord. Every purchase comes with - Will this be worth moving? It is easier to treat the place as if you are passing through it.

But, I don’t buy that.

This guide is my case for caring regardless of the fact.

Hi, I’m Saurabh - a product designer & a fellow tenant based out of Bangalore. I’ve rented for years, and I know the feeling of standing in a room that’s yours but never quite feels like home. The difference comes from understanding how a space works, and making a series of small, intentional decisions that make it feel put together.

Most interior design advice assumes you own the place. It expects you to knock down walls, replace kitchens, install built-ins, and spend accordingly. This guide assumes the opposite. It assumes you rent. Your deposit matters. Your landlord has rules. You’ll probably move again one day. And you’d still like to build a home you’re excited to come back to. Because a temporary home is still your home.

Start slowCopy link

You’re about to set foot in a new space of thought, which is full of decisions which you’re not even sure about yet. Just take it easy. It’s best to enjoy the process and cut yourself a little slack on trying to do better.

Maybe you’re moving in this weekend, or maybe you’re two years into the lease and the room has finally bothered you into action. This guide works the same either way. Even if you rent a single room in a shared flat, your home is just one door smaller. Everything here still applies.

Don’t furnish it all in one weekendCopy link

Moving into a new house comes with the feeling of new beginnings and it’s easy to get swept away by the desire to buy everything at once just so the house feels lived in. I would recommend against it, not because it doesn’t get the desired result, but because you might end up with pieces that don’t fit together. Setting up a home takes time. It is best to buy things when you’re relatively sure about them.

The best reason to wait are natural interactions. In your first weeks the house will teach you things no plan could, because they’re specific to you. Like which corner of the sofa you always end up in, which wall you keep staring at, and where your keys end up everyday. Things bought with this information will fit your home much better than the things bought on day one.

When you’re ready to buy, start with the functional pieces - the bed, the storage, the everyday things. The decorative layer sticks much better on a house that’s already functional. But first, get a measuring tape.

Measure twice, buy onceCopy link

Furniture is expensive and it does not help the heart when you wait a month for your sofa to arrive and it’s just…too big. With your measuring tape, get a good idea of your space and before buying any furniture check and recheck if the piece would fit as you expect.

Don’t skip this by any measure.

Try out second handCopy link

Not everything in your home needs to arrive in a box with a manual. Second hand furniture is cheaper and often better made than what you would get new at the same price. Keep an eye on marketplace listings (like facebook marketplace), thrift stores and hopefully with a little patience, you’ll find pieces that you’ll want to keep forever.

Second hand also makes sense when the piece you really want isn’t affordable yet. A used stopgap has already lost the value it was ever going to lose, so when you upgrade later you can sell it for closer to what you paid.

The only thing I’d say deserves to be bought new is your mattress.

Buy for the next houseCopy link

You’re renting, which means someday you’ll move. Moving day sorts everything you own into two piles - what comes with you, and what stays behind. Freestanding furniture, the kind that stands on its own legs, comes with you. Anything built into the walls might need to stay as a gift to the next tenant. I recommend going for the one which will retain its value in your next house.

This is also the kinder way to think about your first place. You’re not building your forever home in one go. You are collecting pieces that will follow you from house to house, growing with you.

You’ll be unsatisfiedCopy link

And that’s okay, it’s part of the process. I love my current space but I do feel from time to time that things can be better. The desk isn’t getting enough light or my bedside table is a pain to use. Your needs and preferences will change, so your home should change with it. Be more open to this feeling and you’ll find that there are always things you can do to make your space better, while enjoying the process.

There are no rulesCopy link

This is a bit of a contradiction. Approach this guide as a set of best practices. There is no right or wrong way to do this. If your space looks beautiful, is comfortable and brings you peace, the job is done. Don’t let anyone else tell you otherwise.

ConstraintsCopy link

Before you can make a house yours, you have to know what the house is. So this first chapter buys nothing and moves nothing. We’ll just look around to see what we’re working with. Every chapter after this relies on what you notice here, so it’s worth to spend a few minutes.

Rental rulesCopy link

Buy a house and you can do whatever you like to it. Rent one and a set of terms and conditions come attached. Breaking them usually means losing part of your deposit, which is not great.

A few of the common ones -

  • You can’t damage the walls without permission. Usually that means no drilling holes.
  • You can’t damage what came with the house, the appliances, the cupboards, all of it. If it breaks on your watch, it’s on you.
  • You can’t make structural changes. Please don’t knock down any walls.

The short version - you need to hand the house back at the end of the lease in the state you got it in, or close enough that a fresh coat of paint covers the difference.

The lease is the baseline, not the ceiling. Landlords say yes to small changes far more often than you’d think, especially the ones that leave the place better than you found it. Want to put up a shelf, paint a wall, swap a fixture? Ask. The worst case is a no, but asking doesn’t cost anything.

On moving day while the house is still empty, photograph it. Once done, you can forget it but it’ll help make the deposit conversation at the end of your lease to be short and boring, which is exactly what you want it to be. Also, whenever you swap something out, keep the original in a box. When shifting again, the swap comes with you and the original goes back up.

House designCopy link

This is where it starts getting interesting.

Walk the empty house and notice what it came with. What colour are the walls? What are the windows and doors like? Which lights are already fitted, what does the bathroom look like, and what spaces does the house allow to exist at all?

That last question is the one this section is really about. Every home arrives with a stack of decisions already made for you. The plumbing has decided where the kitchen goes. The wardrobe and the AC have decided which room is the bedroom. A socket beside a window is all but pointing at a desk. You can argue with these decisions, but I recommend going along with them because it’s free and more convenient in the longer run. And honestly, that’s good news. These are your constraints, and constraints are the invisible scaffolding of good design.

So, how do you read a house? Say yours has white walls, wood accents and off-white tiles. White is already doing you a favour - light walls and floors make a room feel bigger, and the wood gives the white something warm to lean on. Just clock what the house has decided for you - the base is white, the accent is warm wood. Later when you’re choosing colours, you’ll find the house already did half the work to help you decide.

Do that reading in every room. What’s decided, what’s being suggested, what’s still yours to choose? That’s your starting point.

SunlightCopy link

You can work around almost anything in a rented place. Ugly floor, rug over it. Sad knobs, swap them. However nothing you do can move a window. The sunlight your home gets is the one thing you genuinely cannot change, which makes it worth understanding so you can make the most out of it.

The sun rises in the east and sets in the west, but it never climbs straight overhead. If you're in the Northern Hemisphere, the sun spends the whole day leaning a little to the south, tracing an arc across that side of the sky. So, the south-facing wall in your home stays steadily bright. In the Southern Hemisphere, the sun leans north instead so the north-facing one gets the brightest. Everything below in this section assumes the northern half of the world, so if you're reading this from below the equator, just flip north and south as you go.

South-facing gets steady, warm light all day. This is your prize room. Put the plants, your desk, or the spot where you sit the most, in here. North-facing gets cool, soft, even light that doesn’t glare. Lovely and calm, suitable for screens, for a workspace or a bedroom - just don't expect it to warm you up. East-facing gets gentle morning light and then goes weak by afternoon. West-facing gets nothing all morning and then a hot, low, orange blast in the evening. Beautiful at golden hour, harsh on a laptop screen, and it can turn a small room into an oven in summer.

Watch your home for a day and glance at each room in the morning, at midday, and in the evening. Plants will survive where the sun pools the longest. The darkest room will need the most help later while setting up lighting. The furniture will bleach where the sun strongly sits for hours.

If your home turned out darker than you’d hoped - not a problem. Some of the cosiest rooms I’ve ever been in get no direct sun at all, they were just lit well on purpose.

Power supplyCopy link

Anything that glows, spins or charges needs a socket. And sockets, like windows, don’t move.

Have a look at where the sockets are, and note the other service points too - the internet point, the TV point, the washing machine point, all the 16A ones. A long wall with no socket can’t hold a TV without a wire crossing the room. If nothing reaches the spot where you picture the bed, your phone will spend the whole lease charging on the far side of the room.

Extension boards can fill some of the gaps but mind where the wires end up running, because a cable across a doorway can trip you and it also doesn’t look good. If you really need a socket somewhere, an electrician can also add one on the wall, but that’s a change to the house, so ask the landlord before anyone starts drilling.

FunctionalityCopy link

Last chapter was about what the house gives you, this one is about what you need from it. It matters more than the pretty stuff, even though the pretty stuff is what we all daydream about. A room that fights your daily life stops feeling beautiful really quickly, because it triggers you every time you use it.

The life you liveCopy link

Be honest about how you actually live - not how you’d like to live - how you live. Why? Because space and money are both finite, and every piece of furniture spends some of each. Do you work from home enough to need a desk, or do you mostly work from the sofa? A desk you don’t use is a very expensive tray, and it’s taking space that something else could have had. Do you eat at a table, or on the sofa in front of the TV? Plenty of happy people never own a dining table. Do you want the biggest bed that fits, or would you rather keep the walking space around it? None of these have a right answer, but you need to be honest about these with yourself.

The trap is furnishing an imaginary life. The reading chair is for the reader you’re planning to become. The guest bed is for guests who visit twice a year. We buy these things because they look like the person we’d like to be, and then they sit there, holding down a major chunk of the room and serving nobody. Design for the life you have, the imaginary life can buy its own furniture once its need turns up.

Walk your walkwaysCopy link

The most important but hidden rule in interior design - you should be able to walk your daily routes, bed to bathroom, door to kitchen, sofa to balcony, without turning your shoulders or cramping through.

You walk these routes every day. If one of them makes you shuffle sideways past the dining table, you pay that tiny tax dozens of times a day, every day, for the length of the lease, and it adds up to a home that feels cramped no matter how nice it looks. A clear walkway, on the other hand, is invisible but efficient.

Furniture needs room to be used, not just room to exist. A dining chair needs space behind it to slide out, a chest of drawers needs its own depth again in front of it (or the bottom drawer never fully opens). A bed jammed against a wall on one side means someone needs to climb over the other person every morning.

Have a general idea of which walkways you need to keep clear at all times. Try walking around the house going from one point to the other and your body will default to following the walkways. You can draw a rough map of these walkways to use later, or keep them in mind when you start setting up furniture.

Everything needs an addressCopy link

Every little thing you have needs a place that isn’t a visible surface. Most clutter isn’t about owning too much. Clutter happens when objects have nowhere specific to go, so they settle on the counter, or whatever is closest. When everything has an address, the room can reset in ten minutes.

Some ideas that can help -

  • Multipurpose furniture - Floor space is scarce, so furniture that can also act as storage are very space efficient. A bed with drawers under it, an ottoman that opens into a box, or a bench by the door that can hide shoes. Ignore this if you’ve a huge house.
  • High cupboards - Storage doesn’t always have to sit on the floor. Cupboards can go well above eye level, and the high ones are perfect for stuff you rarely use like your suitcase or your winter things.
  • Flexible storage - Keep some storage open and some closed. Open shelves are for things you like looking at like books or your sentimental possessions. Closed storage are for the cables, tupperware, paperwork which do not need to be out in the open. All open shelving demands constant tidying but closed storage just needs to look good from the outside.

You before guestsCopy link

Start with the honest number - how many people do you actually host, and how often? And design for that instead of the housewarming. A sofa built for eight that seats eight only once a year, is honestly just an expensive laundry station.

The best move is seating that multiplies on demand. For example, stools that stack in a corner or the desk chair that comes to the living room for an evening. An overnight guest needs a decent mattress but maybe not a whole room catered just for that. The better option here can be a sofa which can turn into a bed.

You are the client. A home that can seat twelve but serves one person badly is not a great home. Guests are welcome visitors, and they’ll be more comfortable in a place that feels natural instead of staged.

InspirationCopy link

So far we’ve been sensible. The house is surveyed and the needs are honest. Now we have something to daydream about - what should this place feel like?

This chapter is about finding a direction. The hundred small decisions ahead on what to buy get so much easier when something is helping you decide them for you. Let’s find that something.

You can’t install a styleCopy link

A design style is just a name for choices that tend to go together.

  • Scandinavian - light wood, white walls, soft textiles, calm.
  • Industrial - metal, dark tones, raw unfinished surfaces.
  • Mid-century modern - warm wood, slim legs, shapes from the sixties that still look like the future.
  • Bohemian - layered rugs and cushions, plants everywhere, nothing matching and that being the point.
  • Minimalism - few things, chosen with intent.

Are the names worth knowing? Yes - they’re search terms. Type “mid-century bedside table” into a marketplace and you’ll get things that go together, without having to know why they go together.

But the reality is that you cannot install a style in a rented house. Those flawless Scandinavian flats were photographed in places where they controlled everything from the floor to the switches. You control almost none of that because your house has already decided a lot. No amount of shopping can turn them Scandinavian.

Treat styles like a menu - something to order from, not something to become. Your house should speak its own unique style.

Three words are enoughCopy link

So if not a style, then what? Just pick two or three words. “Warm and calm” “Bright and playful” “Dark, green and bookish” That’s your direction, and its entire purpose is to help you judge the small decisions. Whenever you think about buying something for your house, go back to these words. You’re in a shop holding a lamp, unsure. Warm and calm? Bring the soft fabric shade comes home, not the the pink neon sign. Bright and playful? Maybe get the neon sign.

You don’t have to conjure these words from nowhere, because you probably have the answer already. Look at the things you’d grab first in a fire - like your guitar or the jacket you’ve had for years. They’re a record of your taste that you’ve been keeping with yourself. Whatever they have in common is your direction. You might need to spend some time with them to find the common thread but that’s the first place to begin.

This is the advantage you have over the magazine flats. You don’t arrive at a blank canvas, you arrive with boxes. You can build your home out of what’s in those boxes and it’ll feel like you, because it came from things that already were part of your life.

But if you are starting from scratch and don’t own anything yet - try to gather photos of the things you like. Your can get them from your gallery or from the internet. And repeat the same process, try to find what they have in common and extract just three words out of them.

Steal elements, not roomsCopy link

Let’s go into more detail. You’re already collecting inspiration - the next step is in understanding what you do with it. When an image stops you, ask what in that image stopped you. The room in your reference photo might have different bones from yours. You can’t have the room but you can have the element which caught your eye.

Sometimes it’s that element which is carrying the whole weight, but we attribute it to the whole space doing the work. So steal elements, not rooms. A saved image is a clue about what you like. You’ll keep finding these clues from time to time, keep saving them and when it’s time, make a move.

Break the patternCopy link

What makes a space interesting, rather than just tidy? The answer is contrast. A room gets interesting wherever something breaks the pattern, and there are three channels to break it through - colour, shape, and texture.

Colour is the obvious one, an unexpected colour on a familiar thing. A mustard armchair in a grey room. An orange sofa against a white wall.

Most furniture is straight lines and right angles, so anything that isn’t that, gets noticed because the shape is different - like an arched floor lamp.

Texture is why some all-beige rooms feel rich while others feel like a waiting room. Texture is what a surface promises your hands. Leather, linen, rattan, wool, rough wood are great examples of textures which feel rich. Two rooms with the exact same colours can feel completely different because one has six textures in it and the other has one.

The best pieces carry two channels at once. An orange leather sofa is a colour move and a texture move in a single object, which is why it works perfectly.

But interesting things need calm around them. If everything in the room is special, nothing is. One or two pattern-breakers against a subtle background will always work better than everything fighting for attention.

Third person viewCopy link

A strange fact about living somewhere is that after a couple of weeks, you stop seeing it. Your brain files the whole place under “background” and moves on. That’s why the pile on your chair goes invisible, and it’s also why you stopped noticing the things you got right.

So how do you see your own home again? You borrow someone else’s eyes. Stand outside your door, close your eyes for 30 seconds and walk in like a guest. Stop at the door, and notice what the room says first. Whatever your eye lands on is what the room is currently about. Photos are even better at this. Some day you’ll photograph a corner to sell something or show a friend, and the picture will hand you a room you barely recognise, flaws and all.

Keep this trick close because it’s our feedback loop. The next two chapters are ideas, and the third person view is how you’ll know whether they’re working. Not “do I like this”, you’re biased, of course you do. But what would one see, walking in for the first time?

LightingCopy link

Lighting is the biggest change you can make for the least money and the least permission. You may never repaint, you can’t touch the tiles, but two well-placed lamps will change how a room feels more than either of those would. Best part - most lamp walks out the door with you.

In the daytime your best light is free. The windows are a channel for the sun, and your only job is deciding how they enter your space, which is the next section. It’s the nights that need a plan.

Most homes deal with night using one bright bulb or tubelight on the wall. The whole problem is that one small, harsh source of light is being asked to do what the enormous, soft sky did all day. It can’t. So the room ends up brightly lit but flat and creating shadows in awkward places.

This is where lamps come in, and it helps to understand what a lamp is actually doing. A bare bulb is a hard little point you can’t directly look at. The shade around it diffuses the light, which means it spreads and softens it, turning that harsh point of light into a glow. At night, a few lamps around the room can easily take over the window’s job, but with an upgrade - you can decide where the light comes from.

Get some curtainsCopy link

Sheer curtains - the thin, translucent white ones work like a lampshade for the whole window. Harsh direct sun arrives as an even glow, and you also get privacy without giving up the light. With blackout curtains you add a second, heavier layer over the top which is perfect for blocking light out when it’s time to sleep.

Curtains are usually the largest piece of fabric in a room, so their colour and texture set a good chunk of its mood. This is also a great place to start applying your two or three words from the last chapter.

Diffuse and decentralizeCopy link

You already know diffuse - soft, spread-out light is better than a hard, bare point. Decentralize means not keeping all your light at the same spot. Spread it around the room in several smaller sources instead.

Why does that work? Because a single overhead light leaves the corners in shadow, and your eye reads the shadows as the edges of the room, so the dark corners pull the walls inward. Several smaller lights at different heights fill the room more evenly and bring depth to the space.

As a rule of thumb, most rooms start to feel right at around three points of light, though a small room can be perfectly happy with two. Count how many lamps your favourite café is working with some time. It’s usually more than you’d guess.

Three kinds of lightCopy link

People who light rooms for a living sort light into three jobs -

  • Ambient light is the general fill, the light you simply exist in - soft, overall, coming from no particular direction. Usually uplighters are great for this task because they throw majority of the light upwards at the ceiling.
  • Task light is for doing a specific thing - like the desk lamp or the bedside reading light. They are usually smaller and a room can house more than one of them.
  • Accent light points at a thing rather than illuminating the entire space - a small lamp lighting a plant corner or a soft glow across a bookshelf. Its only job is to give the eye somewhere nice to rest. It creates depth in a room by adding contrast and shadow, drawing the eye directly towards specific points.

A room wants all three and the easiest way to get there is to build them in that order. Add your ambient light first, a soft lamp to light up most of the room. Then add task light wherever you work like your desk. Once these are sorted, add accent lights for wherever you want to pull the eye for depth

Warm and lowCopy link

Buy warm bulbs. Bulb temperature is measured in kelvin, printed on the box as a number with a K after it. The lower the number the warmer the light. Around 2700K is the soft, yellowish light you want at home.

Why warm, though? Because we are adapted to it. For almost all of human history, evening light meant fire and a low sun, both warm. Cool blue light is midday light - the kind that tells your body to stay alert. A warm bulb helps a room feel like somewhere to wind down and relax. Most rooms that feel uneasy at night are just running bulbs that are too cold, too bright, or both.

A lot of the difference between a restaurant and a canteen is simply at what height they put the light. The same brightness feels intimate at sitting height but institutional from the ceiling.

Which lamps to getCopy link

The shape and position of a lamp decide where its light sits in the room. And since getting light at different heights is a big part of making a room feel layered, it helps to know what each kind is good at.

  • The overhead light is usually the one you get with the house, throwing light down from the ceiling. On its own, it's the flat, single-source problem we talked about earlier. So you'll mostly work around it rather than rely on it - put in a warm bulb, and let the lamps below do most of the work. The exception is a pendant lamp - a ceiling light that hangs low on a cord. When dropped over a table, it keeps the light close to the surface and creates a nice pool around it. That's why the dining table is one place where a ceiling light works really well.
  • The floor lamp is the workhorse. If you're only buying one lamp, buy this. It stands tall, requires no custom setup and easiest to move around. There are two useful kinds to know. An uplighter throws light towards the ceiling, filling a whole corner in soft ambient light. An arc or reading lamp reaches out over a chair and points the light down, giving you task light exactly where you need it.
  • The table lamp sits roughly around your height when you're sitting down, which happens to be where warm light feels most intimate. This is the cosy-maker, the lamp that makes an evening feel softer. Put one on a side table, shelf, desk or bedside. Most rooms need at least one.
  • The wall lamp gives you light without taking floor area. That's especially useful beside a bed or above a picture. The catch is that it usually needs mounting and wiring. This means either a conversation with the landlord or a plug-in version with a cord running down the wall.

Where lamps goCopy link

Ask two questions when trying to place a lamp - what is the light for, and what height is the room missing? A dark corner wants a tall floor lamp. A chair you read in wants task light that's close and low.

Start with where the sockets are, because a lamp can only go where the power reaches. If a desired spot is stranded with no socket near it, an electrician can add one on the wall, but that’s a change to the house, so get your landlord involved. They’ll often say yes, since it leaves the place better wired than they found it.

Within the sockets you’ve got, these three placements will be the best to start with -

  • Dark corners - Lighting a corner which blends in the background is the simplest way to make a whole room feel deeper.
  • Where you sit the longest - It can be either right beside you at sitting height to create a cozy space or at standing height so it lights up your book.
  • Behind your screens - If you watch in the dark, a small light can soften the transition from the bright screen & the dark background.

Keep an eye on height as you add lamps. If every light ends up at the same level, the room starts to feel flat again. One tall, one mid, maybe one low. That's often enough to give the room the depth.

You don’t need to kill every shadow, so don’t go overboard with it. Rooms lit in warm pools feel deeper and cosier than rooms lit evenly everywhere. The shadow is part of what makes the light look good.

Dark flatsCopy link

Let’s say you end up with a home without much natural light. We can tackle it in two parts -

  1. Keep the daylight you do get. Use sheer curtains instead of heavy curtains, nothing tall placed in front of the window, and a clear windowsill. A mirror placed where the window can reach it will bounce some of that light further into the room. This will not be much, but you’ll be making the most out of what you’re getting.
  2. Stop chasing the sun, because you will not out-lamp it. Pushing brighter bulbs into a dark room will get you a bright room that feels clinical. So go the other way. Commit to warmth. Think of the coffee shops and bars you never want to leave. Most of them get no real daylight at all, and they feel wonderful - warm bulbs, several small sources, shadows left alone. And it’s okay to turn the lamps on at noon if you feel like it, I’ve done so myself.

Now you know all about lighting, so let’s put it all together.

CompositionCopy link

Everything so far has been about parts - what the house gives you, what you need from it, the direction you chose, how to light it. This chapter is about arrangement, putting those parts into the room so the whole thing feels considered instead of accidental.

Composition has an order to it, and it gets easier the moment you stop seeing a hundred separate objects and start seeing a few kinds of thing. But first, plants -

PlantsCopy link

Plants can be placed in almost any room. They soften the hard straight lines that usually come with furniture. They bring life to a corner and a tall plant can buy you the required height cheaply.

A dark corner wants something that lives off low light like a snake plant or a pothos. A bright windowsill can take plants that thrive in the strong sun. Buy for the light you’ve got, not the plant you fell for, because a thriving cheap plant does more for a room than an expensive one withering in the wrong spot. And if keeping things alive isn’t your gift, a good fake in the right place is fair game.

Big to smallCopy link

Nearly everything in a room is one of four things - furniture, lamps, plants, and your stuff (the personal bits and pieces). Furniture builds the structure, lamps set the mood, plants soften it, and your stuff makes it yours.

Now arrange them in that order, biggest first. Because the big pieces are the hardest to move and the most boxed in by the room, so they should get to decide the layout, and everything smaller arranges around them.

So where does the sofa land? You’ve mostly answered that already. You know where the good daylight falls. You know where the power reaches. You know which paths have to stay clear. You also know which space is the most suited to be a living room. The sofa goes where these four agree and make sense. I do recommend that you measure your pieces and try to draw things out to understand what works or doesn’t work before moving things. There might be situations where you have to make tradeoffs between a few layout options, in which case - just try them out.

Rooms within roomsCopy link

Zoning means giving one room more than one job without building any walls. It matters most to tenants, who often need a single room to be bedroom, office and living room all at once.

Our eyes will happily accept a separate zone, as long as something marks its edge. A rug draws a boundary on the floor, so the seating sitting on it is perceived as one zone. The back of a sofa becomes a soft wall between the living area and the rest. Even a change of light like one lamp-lit corner against a brighter one on the other side, tells you you’ve moved from one zone to another.

You don’t need all of these at once. One or two clear edges are usually enough to turn a single room into two zones that feel independent enough.

The too-big sofaCopy link

You spot a big, deep, inviting sofa in a showroom the size of a tennis court, you bring it home, and it’s just too big for the room. Everything else gets shoved to the walls, and somehow the place feels smaller than it did empty.

In a small room, bigger isn’t more generous. It’s less. One well-sized piece with a little breathing room around it beats a piece that fills the whole room. This is what the measuring tape was for.

The opposite mistake is just as real - a scatter of small pieces, none of them anchoring anything. A room wants one or two things with real presence, and then the smaller things gathered around them.

Empty on purposeCopy link

A room where every surface is occupied feels loud, even when each thing on them is lovely on its own. Empty space is not a gap waiting to be filled, it’s part of the composition.

The instinct, especially in a new place, is to keep adding until it feels finished. But more often a room feels finished when you stop a little sooner than you expected to. The space you leave around the things you love is what makes them seen as chosen, instead of piled.

First thing you seeCopy link

Focal point is the first thing your eye lands on when you walk into a room. It’s a place for someone’s attention to settle. Without it our eyes just wander, and the room feels restless, confusing, or strangely forgettable. It's the visual anchor that gives the room structure and tells your brain where to start looking.

Sometimes the house gives you the focal point for free in the form of a big window with a view or a fireplace. When it doesn’t, you can choose what it is by using size, contrast or height. It can be your bed in the bedroom or a piece of art in your hall.

How do you find a focal point? Stand in the doorway and notice where your eye goes first - this is your focal point. You see your own home mostly from the few places you settle into like the sofa. So once the doorway view works, go and sit where you usually sit, and look again. Based on different positions across the room there can be multiple focal points, so you need to be careful that the focal point from one position is not fighting with another position’s focal point. In these situations, sometimes less is better.

Colour ruleCopy link

When thinking about colors in a space, there’s a simple 60-30-10 rule that keeps colour from tipping into either boring or chaotic.

Roughly 60% of the room should be your dominant colour - like the walls or the floor. About 30% should be a secondary colour supporting it - like an armchair or the curtains. The final 10% is your accent, usually the loud one, should be in small doses - like in cushions, a vase, or an art piece. The accent works only because there’s so little of it. An orange sofa is a great accent. Four orange things in one room is a warning sign.

Colors also help in making a room look pulled together. How? By repeating your accent instead of stranding it in one spot. A single red cushion looks random, like it was placed there by accident. Pick that red up again in a book spine and one trinket, and suddenly it looks deliberate. It’s like creating a theme. The same goes for texture, a little wood repeated in a few places ties everything together.

You don’t need to accurately measure any of this, by the way. It’s a rule of thumb for a feeling - most of the room subtle, some of it supporting, a little of it loud, but echoed across the space.

BalanceCopy link

Balance is how visual weight sits across a space. Put a heavy wardrobe and a stuffed bookshelf in one corner and a single lamp in the far one, and the room leans towards one side. You'll feel it before you can put your finger on it.

The tidy option is keeping a matched pair - two identical lamps on two bedside tables, one each side of the bed. It looks calm and a little formal, like it was planned. The looser option is to answer one weight with a different weight - a tall bookshelf on one wall against a floor lamp and a big plant on the other. They won’t match, but the two sides will balance each other, and that's the version that feels more lived-in. Neither is more right, just go with the mood you want.

Height works the same way, but people realize it after everything is placed. Picture a room where the sofa, the coffee table and the sideboard all sit between your knee and your hip. Everything at one level. Why does that fall flat? Because there's nothing for your eyes to climb, so it slides straight across and out. The fix is things vary in height - something tall like a shelf or a floor plant, something in the middle like the sofa, something low like the center table in front of it. Now the eye moves up and down the room, and the space feels fuller and balanced.

Groups and anchorsCopy link

Objects look better gathered than scattered. Three things clustered at one end of a table look deliberate. The same three spaced evenly around the room look like they were set down and forgotten. So when in doubt, group things, by theme, by colour, or just by placing them near each other.

A group needs something to hold it together, an anchor, so it doesn’t feel cluttered. A tray gathers the clutter on a table into one intentional little island. On a bigger scale, a rug anchors a whole seating arrangement in your living room. For rugs, there’s a rule that might help you buy the right size - at the very least, the front legs of the sofa and chairs should sit on the rug. Avoid having a small rug stranded in the middle with all the furniture off it, floating there like a bath mat placed in the wrong room.

My own living room is built in a way where a rug wouldn't help with anything. Of its three walls, two are lined with low cabinets and the sofa takes up the third. The built-in furniture already anchors the space, so a rug would just be a thing on the floor.

Rule of threesCopy link

Odd numbers arrange more easily than even ones. Two objects tend to square off like bookends but three settle into something the eye simply enjoys, especially if you stagger their heights so they make a loose triangle instead of a straight line. It’s the quickest way to dress any surface you’re stuck on - be it a shelf, a coffee table, or a bedside. Three things of different heights with something in common will nearly always look composed so it’s a reliable place to start when an arrangement feels off.

You can work with less or more pieces too, but then it’s all upto you to figure out how they go together. The best part is that this is something you can figure out with time and can also be a fun activity for you and your friends.

Make it feel biggerCopy link

Bigger spaces always feel more comfortable because they provide physical freedom. Living in a small space feels cozy, but it gets overcrowded if your furniture is working against it. To get the best of both worlds, a few small moves make a room feel bigger than it actually is -

Keep the floors visible. The more unbroken floor the eye can follow, the larger the room feels. So anything that keeps the floor in view is working for you. Furniture on legs lifted even four or five inches off the ground lets the floor run on underneath it, which makes the space feel bigger. Things you can see through do the same job, like a glass or acrylic tabletop, an open shelving, or a chair with a slim frame.

Mirrors extend the visible space. They bounce the light around but it also repeats whatever sits in front of it, so a room with a good mirror is perceived more bigger than it is. Placement is the whole game here. Lean or hang a large mirror opposite or just beside a window and it doubles the light, the view and the perceived space depending on the angle.

Continuity helps make things belong with each other. Every extra colour and material divides the room into different parts, and a room composed of many small parts feels smaller. So keep the big surfaces like the walls, floor, curtains and big furniture, in tones that sit closer together, and let colours dominate the small stuff like the cushions, artwork or lamps. Curtains with colors closer to the wall colour make the wall seem to carry on behind them. They all don’t need to match exactly, it just shouldn't fight with each other.

And lastly, keep things tidy. Every object on show is one more thing the eye has to stop and account for, so a floor and counters filled with them looks busy, which the brain treats them as small. Get the cables down behind the furniture or into a box. Keep the floor and most of each surface clear, and the room opens up without adding a single thing. Try it yourself, if you have a pile of laundry on your bed, the bed will look smaller than it is. Nothing shrinks a space faster than the mess the eye has to keep working around.

Rotate your stuffCopy link

Your personal possessions add a lot of personality and character to your space. The books, the photos, the guitar, the souvenirs, the small stuffed rabbit you've had for years. Everything up to now has built a nice room. Your things are what make it yours instead of a showroom.

The trap is putting it all out at once. A shelf crammed with everything leans towards clutter, and the pieces you love get lost in the crowd. Pick a handful, group them the way we just talked about, and keep the rest. Swap them in whenever you have the mood, there's no right number and no schedule. Swapping also refreshes the space, which makes you feel the sense of “new” without the expense.

Walls & artCopy link

A wall is an abundant real estate to put up the things that feel like you, but there are two easy ways to get it wrong - too small, and too high. A single frame alone on a big wall looks lost. Either go bigger, or hang a few smaller pieces together so they cover about the same ground as one large piece would. People usually place stuff too high on their walls, which makes it feel disconnected from the space. Aim for the middle of the piece to sit around eye level, a touch lower than what feels right, so it belongs to the room instead of floating above it.

When art hangs above or around a bigger piece like a sofa, bed or console, line it up with that piece so the two feel like one arrangement. Centre the art, or the group, over the furniture rather than off to one side. Just don't let lining it up make you place it too high, the eye-level rule still comes first.

The good news for tenants is you often don't need to drill a hole at all. A large piece leaned against the wall, on the floor or along a shelf, looks relaxed and deliberate. A picture ledge or a narrow shelf holds a row of frames you can swap around whenever you like, no fresh holes each time.

Smell & soundCopy link

A home is more than something to look at. This last layer is the one you feel rather than see - a place has a smell and a sound, and both shape what it's like to be in it.

For smell, clean comes first, and after that a candle or some incense, nothing so strong it flushes your senses the moment you’re at the door. For sound, a silent room can feel emptier than it looks, and soft music fills it with a rhythm. The soft materials you've been adding all along like the rug, the curtains, help here by soaking up the hard echo that makes a room feel bare. All this doesn’t show up in a photo but it's a big part of why some homes are memorable.

SpacesCopy link

By now you have the whole method, and it works the same in every space. Survey the constraints, decide the job, pick a direction, light it, compose it. But there are a few caveats -

Each space has its own job and a few specifics, the practical things that are true of a kitchen but not a bedroom, of a desk but not a sofa. Some spaces are also harder than others. The kitchen and bathroom especially, where nearly everything is bolted down and the lighting is usually the worst in the house. We'll get to those. This is a walk through the home, roughly front door inward, so you can skip ahead to the spaces you have.

EntrywayCopy link

Most rentals don't come with a proper entrance, just the first meter inside the door. It’s still worth designing because it's the first thing you see coming home and the last thing you see on the way out.

It wants three small things. Somewhere to drop your keys like a hook or a little dish, so they stop going missing around the house. Somewhere for shoes like a low rack or a mat, so they don't pile up against the wall. And a mirror, which is doing a lot at once - it checks your face on the way out, it bounces light into what's usually a dark strip, and it makes a tight hallway feel less tight. Add one warm bulb or a small lamp, and it starts to feel like arriving somewhere special.

Living roomCopy link

The living room needs to work more than any other room in most flats. It's where you unwind, where guests end up, and often where you eat and sometimes work too. Its job is to allow people to come together, but the most common pitfall is building the whole thing around a television.

Point the seating at each other, not just at the screen. Conversation happens at angles, so a sofa and a chair turned slightly towards one another makes it more comfortable. The TV wants to be the focal point, and it turns into a black rectangle the moment it's off. You don't have to hide it, just don't let it be the only thing on its wall. Give it a shelf, some art, or a plant beside it, so your eyes have somewhere else to land.

The sofa is your biggest piece and your anchor, so it goes down first, facing whatever you've chosen as the focal point. If this room is also your dining space or your office, this is where zoning can help you out. A rug under the seating, a shelf behind the sofa, a lamp for each area, and one room transcends into two.

WorkspaceCopy link

If you work from home, your workspace deserves real thought, because you spend more waking hours there than almost anywhere else. If you do need a workspace, here's what it specifically needs -

Good lighting, because a screen makes the lighting scenario a little different. Daylight from the side of the screen is the sweetest spot. A bright window straight ahead behind your screen fights your display and you won’t be able to see it clearly without maximum brightness. This can be avoided if you use curtains on a window that gets less light. A window directly opposite to the screen throws glare across it. So ideally set the desk with a window to one side. At night, a task lamp from that same side does the same job.

You stare at whatever sits in front of the desk for hours, so give it a wall you've made nice with art, plants, books or trinkets. I personally prefer a minimal desk because it helps me focus, so all the nice stuff is on the walls. If you can, keep the desk out of the bedroom, because your brain rests better when work and sleep happen in different places. But if it has to share the bedroom, angle it away from the bed, or screen it off with a shelf, so you can shut the working day by the evening.

If the room just has a desk and a chair, it will sound like a batcave during online meetings. From the sound of your speakers but also for your mic, making it harder for the people across the screen to hear your properly. Invest in soft surfaces which can absorb that echo like a sofa or a seating space. Putting stuff on the wall and adding extra surfaces like shelves can also help with the echo while adding character to your video background.

BedroomCopy link

This is the most personal room in the house. Its job is rest, so everything in it should be working toward sleep.

The bed is the biggest piece and the natural focal point, so it goes in first. Where you can, place the bed so you see the door from it without being jammed right up against it. Keep a note of where the sun falls in the room so it’s not a nuisance in the morning. The light that matters here is a bedside lamp - warm and low - because you use it for winding down, not working. If the room needs to go properly dark, layer a blackout curtain over your sheers.

This is also the room that rewards soft texture the most, so it's the place to spend a little on bedding, on a rug for your feet in the morning, and a heavier curtain. It's the room where empty on purpose pays off too - a calm, uncluttered bedroom is easier to fall asleep in than a busy one. Save the interesting clutter for the rooms you're awake in and invest in bedsheets that you love.

KitchenCopy link

A rented kitchen is mostly fixed so work the surface instead of fighting the structure.

Start with the counters and give everything there an address. A clear counter makes the whole kitchen feel bigger and calmer, so the appliances you use once a week go into a cupboard, and only the daily few stay out. Whatever stays out is on display, which means it has to look good, like stuff on any open shelf. Replace the loud packaging of your packed items with a few plain jars, unless the brand packaging really looks great. Let them be the decor.

Because counter space is precious and wall space is free, go vertical. You can use the backsplash tiles to keep the stuff you use everyday - like a magnetic strip for the knives or a few hooks for mugs and pans. It lifts clutter off the counter, and can be installed with adhesive or double-sided tape. If you have enough storage cabinets under the counter, this might not be required.

Most kitchens come with one cold, bright light on the ceiling. Where you're allowed to change the bulb, go warm. Where you're not, a warm strip light facing down at the counter can work really well as accent lighting. Some people prefer to have the natural light to see if they’ve got the right color while cooking. For them, I suggest keeping the bulb you’ve got with the house and go for the warm accent lighting only when you’re not cooking.

A few plants and herbs on the windowsill can soften the kitchen and you can cook with them!

BathroomCopy link

The bathroom is usually small enough that changing a few things make big impacts.

  • The shower head is the classic swap. Unscrew the landlord's, keep it in the box, screw on a better one. Five minutes, a better start to every morning, and completely reversible.
  • Your towels are the colour block. In a small white-tiled bathroom, a set of towels as one of your accent colours does the same job a painted wall would do in a bigger space.
  • A warm bulb over the mirror, keeping it bright enough to see your face.
  • A plant that likes damp and dim light like a pothos or a small fern, is very happy in a steamy bathroom.
  • Fragrance is a big part of making a bathroom feel fresh - place a candle or keep a soap which smells close to something you like.

That’s most of it, we just dressed the bathroom.

BalconyCopy link

If you're lucky enough to have a balcony, treat it as a room. A single chair, a few plants and a warm light for the evening might get you a spot where you’d like to have your morning coffee. Some houses provision the balcony as the space for the washing machine, usually when the balcony is attached to the kitchen. Don’t fight it, place the washing machine, and put a nice cover on top so it looks like part of your plan.

Studio apartmentsCopy link

Some homes are just a single room that needs to do everything. And some tenants have a whole apartment but really only control one room of it. Either way, this is where zoning turns into the whole game.

If you have a studio apartment, divide it by what you do where, so you're not relaxing in your bedroom or sleeping in your living room. Use the rug, the back of a sofa, a shelf or a curtain to mark out the living space. Keep one base tone across the whole room so it reads as a single place and lean hard on the make-it-feel-bigger tricks, because here they will compound.

If you rent a room in a shared flat, put your energy behind your own door. Make that one room genuinely excellent. It's yours, and it's the part of this guide you have full permission to go all in on. The shared spaces can work with one good lamp or a plant in the common room - offered rather than imposed. It goes a long way with flatmates and landlords both. Maybe share this guide with them too, so all of you are on the same page.

It's yours nowCopy link

You've made it to the end. There's one idea I saved for here, because it only makes sense once you've walked every room - let the whole place feel like one place.

Room by room, it's easy to end up with a scandinavian bedroom, an industrial kitchen and a living room that feels like neither of them. What holds them together is the base tone and the background colour of the house. Keep it running from room to room and let only the accents change on top. Let it become a single place you move through, instead of a row of showrooms.

I don't want to hand it all over and leave you thinking there's a finish line, because there isn't one. Your needs will shift, you'll find a better chair, the light moves with the seasons, and you'll shuffle that same plant around three more times before it settles anywhere solid. It doesn’t mean the home is failing. It's a living thing you keep tending to, not a photo you take once and hang on the wall.

So, take it easy. Buy the thing when you're sure of it, sit with the bare wall for a while, and let the place become yours at the pace it wants to. It’s not going to happen in a weekend, and it was never meant to. It's your home, even though it's rented.

Maybe especially because it's rented.
